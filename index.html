WHY I DO THIS

  • I study the gap between what people say and what they do.Most human behaviour runs on autopilot. My interest is in making the invisible visible: finding the patterns in what people do and giving teams something they can actually act on.
  • The counterintuitive questions are the interesting ones.Why does removing a feature players hated make them angrier? Why does anticipating a reward feel different from receiving it, even when the brain responds the same way?
  • Games are a unique research environment.High-stakes emotional behaviour (frustration, trust, flow, social dynamics) in a controlled setting. The stakes feel low to the participant. The insight is anything but.
  • I've experienced gaming communities at scale.Attending major esports events shapes how I think about player psychology, not just as data points, but as something felt firsthand.

VR Cognitive Impairment Simulation

Designed and built a proof-of-concept VR environment in Unreal Engine simulating alcohol-like visual impairment, proposed as part of a larger study into how impairment affects cognitive performance. The prototype demonstrated one of four planned cognitive tests, introducing measurable perceptual distortions during an in-world task with a single demonstration participant.

UNIVERSITY OF SURREY · BSC PSYCHOLOGY · 2025

Motor Learning in DCD: Gamified Research with Children

First Class Honours

Investigated motor learning differences in children with Developmental Coordination Disorder using a gamified visuomotor rotation task, presented as an alien-feeding game for participants aged 9 to 11. Piloted across 6 runs of two 50-trial stages each, the study compared implicit and explicit learning strategies across DCD and typically-developing groups.

UNIVERSITY OF SURREY · MSC DISSERTATION · 2026

Passenger Trust in Autonomous Vehicles

Submitted September 2026

Do explanations change how much passengers trust a self-driving car? In a 2x2 within-subjects online experiment, 43 participants watched four first-person rides filmed in a custom Unreal Engine driving environment: smooth or assertive driving, with or without a calm voice explaining each manoeuvre before it happened (“Slowing due to pedestrian near crossing”). After every ride they rated their trust, how predictable the car felt, and how closely they would watch it.

KEY FINDINGExplanations did the most work where trust was weakest. They lifted trust about two and a half times more after assertive driving than after smooth driving (style × explanation interaction, F(1,42) = 9.31, p = .004). They also made the car feel more predictable in both styles and reduced how closely people intended to monitor it.
SO WHAT FOR DESIGNExplain adaptively rather than uniformly: a vehicle should say more when its behaviour is less comfortable, instead of narrating everything the same way.

Next, I want to run the same design in immersive VR. I am continuing this research beyond the MSc and preparing the findings for publication.

I Usability Tested My Own Portfolio

Just for a little bit of fun.

A researcher's portfolio is a product too. Before launch I ran the same process I'd run for a studio: recruit, test, analyse, fix, retest. Six participants (2 UX researchers, 1 PhD student and 3 MSc peers) completed four tasks while thinking aloud.

HIGHImportant information was difficult to find

Three of six participants struggled to find my availability and location, because it sat too far down the page.

"I'd normally have moved on by now. I just need to know when you can start and where you're based."

FIXAdded a quick facts section to the first screen, so key information is visible straight away. After the change, all six found it, with a median of 5 seconds.

HIGHScrolling between sections wasn't always working

Some trackpad users had trouble scrolling on to the next section, and two participants thought the website had frozen.

FIXMade the scrolling respond more reliably and added a clear "Next stop" button to make moving on easier.

MEDIUMCreative wording caused confusion

The heading "Insert cartridge" wasn't immediately clear to two participants, who needed extra time to understand the section. Both appreciated the playful wording once they got it.

DECISIONKept the heading to hold on to the arcade theme, with a short explanation underneath for clarity.

OUTCOMEThe hardest task went from 3 of 6 to all 6 participants succeeding, and its median time dropped from 82 seconds to 5. Next: prioritise mobile testing, and include recruiters in future sessions for the most relevant feedback.

METHODS USED

  • Think-aloudParticipants talked through their thoughts while navigating the website.
  • Task-based testingSpecific tasks showed how easily people could find information.
  • Affinity mappingGrouping similar feedback revealed the common issues.
  • Severity ratingIssues were prioritised by their impact on the experience.
  • Iterate and retestChanges were tested again to check they worked.

COUNTER-STRIKE 2

A Tale of Two Economies

A psychological critique separating CS2's two design systems: a skill-based in-game economy built on prospect theory and cognitive load, and a monetisation economy built on variable reinforcement, the near-miss effect, and classical conditioning. Examines the ethics of gambling mechanics in a free-to-access platform that peaks at 1.8 million concurrent players.

"The skin economy isn't cosmetic. It's a second game built on variable reinforcement, running parallel to the first."

LEAGUE OF LEGENDS

Mastery, Monetisation, and Manufactured Toxicity

A three-part critique of how League of Legends sustains 180 million players through emergent complexity and staged mastery; how ranked satisfies self-determination needs while weaponising loss aversion and the goal gradient effect; and how Hextech monetisation deploys variable ratio reinforcement and engineered FOMO against a largely adolescent player base.

"Toxicity isn't a player personality problem. Riot built the conditions for it."
